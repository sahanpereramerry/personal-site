On most large corporate websites, content placement relies almost entirely on institutional memory. A draft arrives, and a senior editor or section owner reads it, recalls what the company has published recently, and decides where it fits. The process works well enough at low volumes, but it is opaque, impossible to scale, and fragile. The moment that gatekeeper is on leave—or a new writer joins the team—the system breaks down.

Yet the information required to make those judgements—what has already been published, in which format, under which taxonomy, and how it relates to the wider estate—is already recorded. It sits inside the content management system. The difficulty is that most organisations treat their CMS merely as a publishing mechanism: drafts go in, web pages come out. Almost no one treats it as a queryable dataset.

Changing that premise alters how an editorial team operates. By exporting our company’s CMS records and restructuring them into a local database, I was able to build a set of workflows that answer foundational editorial questions—where a piece belongs, what we already have on the topic, and whether a draft duplicates existing work—at scale and with hard evidence. The aim is not to automate editorial judgement, which remains a human job, but to ensure that when editors sit down to make a decision, they are working from data rather than a hunch.

Why site search fails the editorial test

A corporate web estate of any maturity has an underlying architecture that standard keyword search cannot grasp. The boundaries between industry verticals, partner pages, and service lines are commercial and editorial distinctions, not lexical ones. To the architect of the site, the difference between a piece on ‘AI in software development’ and one on ‘AI in financial services’ is obvious; to a conventional search engine, the two look much the same.

When I tested an automated placement tool that relied on searching our live website, these limitations surfaced immediately. A query regarding a specific subsidiary’s technical work returned broad results from the wider group portfolio, failing to make the corporate distinction the writer needed. Another search in an industry vertical ranked an isolated customer case study above the main hub page, even though the hub was the obvious home for the new draft. Search engines are built to find pages that share vocabulary. They do not understand how a website is organised, nor can they tell you whether a related page is a parent hub, a peer article, or an outdated campaign page.

Building a local mirror

Part of the problem is that our publishing data is split across two systems. The CMS holds the editorial record (metadata, taxonomy, authors, and lifecycle status), while the page-builder holds the live URLs and the layout visitors actually see. Neither system holds the complete picture. The page-builder knows how a live page is assembled, while the CMS knows about assets that were never turned into web pages at all.

To bridge the gap, I built a read-only SQLite database on my laptop that joins both exports together. Where a CMS entry maps to a live URL, the database links them, allowing a user to trace an editorial record straight through to the published page. Where a CMS record has no corresponding web page—such as a standalone PDF report or an archived entry—I deliberately kept it in the index. Excluding those records would have meant wiping legitimate company publications from our institutional memory simply because they lacked a landing page.

The resulting database holds nearly 23,000 records. It pairs traditional full-text search for exact phrasing with vector embeddings that surface conceptually related material even when the terminology differs. Because the entire system runs locally, it requires no live API sessions or engineering maintenance.

How it changes the workflow

Sitting on top of this database are workflows that evaluate incoming drafts in bulk. Instead of submitting articles one by one and waiting for a section editor to review them cold, I can run a batch of drafts against the archive and generate an editorial brief for each.

First, the system identifies where a draft belongs based on structure and meaning. When I tested a case study on infrastructure automation, the workflow pinpointed not just the relevant hub page, but the specific sub-section and taxonomy tag required to feed content into it.

Second, it flags thematic overlap. Rather than killing a commission, discovering an overlap usually sharpens the brief. In one instance, seeing that we had already covered a topic allowed me to go back to the subject-matter expert with a much more specific question: what is the concrete commercial example that makes this new piece distinct from the paper we published six months ago?

Finally, it assesses format, looking at the structure and argument of a draft to determine whether it reads like a case study, a hub contribution, or an opinion piece. The human editor still makes the final call, but the submission arrives with its context already mapped.

Finding what the website hides

Treating the CMS as a dataset also exposes gaps in how a company tracks its own output. Recently, a colleague compiling a vertical newsletter asked what we had published in a particular sector. An earlier content audit, which had been scraped from our live web pages, missed a major white paper. Because the paper existed in the CMS as a direct PDF download without a dedicated landing page in the design system, the web-scraper had walked right past it.

My local mirror caught it immediately because I had chosen to retain unmapped CMS records. In any enterprise CMS of sufficient age, a surprising amount of valuable intellectual property sits disconnected from the front-end navigation. Relying solely on the live website for content audits or gap analyses guarantees an incomplete picture.

The operational takeaway

As corporate publishing volumes grow—accelerated, inevitably, by generative AI—relying on a handful of senior editors to hold the entire site map in their heads is no longer tenable.

Yet the fix does not require an expensive data-engineering project or a new enterprise software procurement. A local database and a set of text embeddings are well within the reach of a technically minded editorial lead. The real hurdle is conceptual: recognising that the hardest part of content operations is editorial judgement, and that the data required to support that judgement is already sitting in a system the company pays for. It simply needs to be read.
