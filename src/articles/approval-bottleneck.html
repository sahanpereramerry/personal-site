AI has made the first draft nearly free. All it takes to create a blog is a rough slide deck, a transcript, or a cluster of project notes. Feed them to the LLM of your choice, and readable copy is generated almost instantly. As a result, companies assume that the bottleneck for content has been removed.

Running content across a group of several dozen specialist businesses taught me otherwise.

When writing was slow and expensive, that friction paced the rest of the organisation. Because producing a draft took actual effort, teams were selective about what they commissioned, and finished drafts trickled into the sign-off queue at a rate that technical experts, account leads, and lawyers could actually absorb.

When you make drafting cheap without changing how decisions get made downstream, the bottleneck moves. In a worst-case scenario, teams start generating plausible drafts in minutes and lobbing them over the fence (or to be more precise, across the open-plan office). These unexamined drafts, which Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke called ‘slop grenades’ on The Knowledge Project podcast in September 2026, inevitably explode as the content has to be rewritten, repurposed, or abandoned. Many people have written about this.

But there is another, more exasperating situation, where the content being sent through is actually good. An influx of content, no matter the quality, still has the ability to clog up the content engine, because the experts, account leads, and lawyers who must verify and approve it do not suddenly have more hours in the day. A 2026 vendor survey from web platform Pantheon points the same way, finding that 31% of marketing leaders now cite internal review and approval as their biggest blocker to getting content live, ahead of content creation itself at 21%. Instead of shipping more, a company is overwhelmed.

Why you can’t just copy software

Software engineering teams have run into the same squeeze. A 2026 study by researchers at Carnegie Mellon and Stanford, tracking roughly 800 developers at an AI-forward company, found that pull-request volume rose 3.1 times while the reviewer pool grew only 1.5 times, roughly doubling the load on each reviewer. Even so, software teams have two advantages marketing does not. First, they can actually see the jam, because every reviewer works inside one system that automatically logs each exchange, version, and wait time. Second, that company eased the pressure by bypassing human review altogether, letting the share of human-reviewed changes drop from 89% to 68% in favour of automated checks.

Corporate marketing has neither escape hatch. You cannot hand a client confidentiality clause to an automated reviewer, and your human reviewers do not live in your tracker. They reply by email, raise caveats on calls, and send back marked-up Word attachments; a single piece can loop back and forth several times between editorial, technical specialists, account leads, and Legal. Worse, an approval often depends on context that has nothing to do with the text itself, such as a sensitive renewal meeting coming up next week. Try to codify that reality into a neat, stage-by-stage project board, and it will be out of date within a fortnight.

Track the one thing that stays true

Complex boards go stale quickly, while overly simple ones hide what is really happening. Whatever stage a piece is supposedly in, however, one question always has an answer: who owes the next decision, and how long have they had it?

Our own tracker originally reflected a simple view of the world: Drafting, In Progress, and Published. At one point, it showed fourteen articles sitting in ‘Drafting’, making it look as though writing speed was still the constraint.

When I audited the actual files, half of those pieces had already been written and handed over to a central reviewer. Because the tracker did not distinguish between producing a draft and waiting for a verdict on it, a review queue was masquerading as an editorial delay. Simply reclassifying those pieces to reflect who owed the next decision, and how long an item had sat in their court, halved the apparent drafting backlog overnight and exposed where work was actually stalling.

Once you separate the editorial work you control from the decisions sitting with other teams, you stop treating every delay as a writing problem. Often the hold-up has nothing to do with the text at all, and once the wait is visible it can be surprisingly simple to remove. On one piece, progress had stalled solely because a client’s signed approval form was sitting unforwarded in an inbox, a blind spot we permanently closed by moving sign-offs into an online signing tool that automatically notified publishing the moment a document was executed. On another, when a relationship owner pushed back against asking a client to sign a fresh form, we cleared the block by digging out an earlier email chain and getting Legal to accept the existing correspondence as sufficient cover.

Ration what enters the queue

You cannot make approval faster, which means that once reviewers are already stretched by publishable work, the worst thing you can put in the queue is a piece that can never go live. The deeper danger of effortless drafting is that it tempts teams to use a finished article to test whether a story is allowed to exist.

Case studies almost always begin with internal momentum: a delivery team finishes a successful project, the day-to-day client contact is complimentary, and someone commissions a draft on the assumption that formal sign-off will be a formality. When spinning up a draft takes very little effort, getting words on the page feels like harmless progress.

On one project, we developed a detailed story with the full backing of the delivery team, putting it through drafting, technical fact-checking, and internal review, only for the client to refuse publication at the final hurdle.

The instinctive fallback inside most marketing teams is to strip out the client’s name and run the piece anonymously. When I took that proposed workaround to Legal, however, it exposed a trap that polished copy easily hides. Even without a company name, the specific combination of technology, geography, and project scale, read alongside information already in the public domain, made the client identifiable. Because our contract restricted publicising the relationship itself, a simple find-and-replace could not save the piece. By pushing an unverified story straight into production, we had burned weeks of specialist and legal review time on an asset that could never go live.

Most dead-end work like this happens because organisations compress four distinct questions into the single word approved:

  1. Does our internal team think this is a strong story?
  2. Is the client contact open to the idea in principle?
  3. Are our technical and editorial reviewers happy with the claims?
  4. Has the client’s organisation formally authorised publication for our intended channels?

When writing was the scarcest resource, teams could sometimes get away with blurring those stages. When review capacity and client goodwill are the scarce resources, you have to invert the workflow and move the client’s decision to the very beginning.

To stop the pipeline from filling up with unpublishable drafts, I changed our rule of engagement: nothing goes into full drafting until we have written confirmation that the client is open to the piece, along with clarity on how it will be used. Introducing that gate does slow down the start, and relationship owners occasionally resist because raising permissions before there is a polished draft to show feels premature. In practice, a brief, proportionate conversation upfront surfaces fatal restrictions while the idea is still free to abandon.

What this does and doesn’t fix

Neither of these changes makes approval fast. Drafting can be automated, whereas commercial and legal liability cannot, so sign-off still runs at human speed through people and conversations that no tracker will ever fully capture.

What they do is show you where the queue actually is and stop you filling it with dead ends. Since bringing that rule in, we have not burned a single week of specialist or legal review on a piece that was later refused by a client.

In an environment where anyone can generate copy, an operator’s job is no longer just feeding the top of the funnel. It is protecting the organisation’s review capacity, making sure you only spend time writing what you can actually ship.
