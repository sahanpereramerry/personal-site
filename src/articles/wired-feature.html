When technology companies pitch the press, they usually lead with what matters to them internally—a new office opening, an executive keynote, or a broad showcase of capabilities. To a journalist scanning an inbox, that breadth reads like corporate noise. Even when a pitch does catch someone's eye, teams often treat the initial reply as the finish line, forgetting that a cold email has to survive being forwarded to a stranger inside the newsroom, and that the real work starts when a reporter decides to pursue a specific story.

During the opening of Reply’s London offices, I ran the outreach for a live showcase of our AI engineering work. Twelve days after I sent 31 invitations, WIRED published a feature on Apollo, an AI model built to help scholars reconstruct damaged Ancient Greek papyri. Looking back at the sequence from the initial cold email to publication, the process relied on three operational decisions: selecting a single distinct asset to carry the pitch, packaging the context so the email could travel on its own, and running a fast evidence workflow once the journalist decided what he actually wanted to investigate.

1. Choosing the single sharpest asset

The event itself was broad. We were inviting press to walk through the "House of Models," a physical space demonstrating how AI systems move from data preparation and specialisation through to evaluation, deployment, and monitoring, alongside access to technical specialists and our UK CEO.

Pitching the entire floor equally would have diluted the invitation. Looking across everything we were showing, one project stood out immediately: Apollo. An AI model trained to help historians read damaged Ancient Greek texts had a subject anyone outside the company could immediately picture—fragments of papyrus, missing words, and researchers trying to recover their meaning. It was an unusual application, and it raised compelling questions even for a reader who did not care about enterprise software architecture.

Instead of listing a dozen demos, I made Apollo the centrepiece of the invitation. I named the model directly, explained what it did, and linked to the initial project announcement so a sceptical reader could verify that the work was real. The broader tour of the AI production floor remained the format of the visit, while Apollo provided the specific hook that made the trip worth a journalist’s morning.

2. Writing an email that survives being forwarded

The WIRED journalist who attended the event and wrote the feature was not the person I originally emailed. I had sent the invitation to one of his colleagues; the next day, the reporter replied to say the message had been passed along to him and that he would like to attend.

When you write cold outreach, it is easy to assume the recipient already knows who your company is, or to rely on a personalised opening line tailored to one specific writer. Had I written the email that way, it likely would have died when forwarded. A second reader inheriting a forwarded thread only has what is on the page.

To make the invitation self-contained, I built three layers of context directly into the body of the email:

  • Concrete action over internal branding: "House of Models" is an internal name that means nothing to an outsider. The subject line and opening paragraph translated it into a plain physical description—"Tue 10:30: walk through a working AI production floor in Victoria"—and mapped out the exact stations a visitor would see.
  • Institutional credibility: Working inside a large organisation makes it easy to forget that a journalist might not recognise the brand. I included a brief, factual summary of Reply as a €2.5 billion technology group, named our major technology partnerships, and identified the specific partners behind Apollo—Mistral and the Austrian Academy of Sciences—with links to the underlying evidence.
  • Clear logistics: The email closed with the exact time, street address, interview availability, and a simple way to accept.

Because all of that context travelled together in a single message, a colleague could forward it across the WIRED newsroom and give the reporter everything he needed to judge whether the visit was worth his time.

3. Supporting the story the journalist actually wants to tell

The first email gets a reporter into the room; the work after the visit determines whether a story can actually be reported in time.

During the tour, the journalist mentioned that he was specifically interested in the Apollo model. The following morning, his email turned that interest into a concrete set of reporting questions. He asked for written source materials, details on the benchmarks and launch, and an interview with a researcher on the project.

At that point, the job shifted from outreach to source gathering. If a team stalls or sends back vague marketing copy when a reporter asks for evidence, the opportunity quickly passes. Over the next 24 hours, I worked with the project team to pull together the relevant public documentation and clarify what we could share ahead of the formal launch.

The following day, I sent the reporter six public sources—including the original project announcement, Mistral’s technical overview, a published interview with the lead researcher, a conference abstract, and documentation for the training corpus. At the same time, I coordinated internally to arrange a research interview, introducing the journalist to Anna Dolganov, the historian and papyrologist leading the Apollo project at the Austrian Academy of Sciences, for a conversation the next day.

The boundary between pitching and reporting

When the feature went live on 22 September, it was a product of the journalist’s independent reporting. Rather than writing about our office opening or the wider production floor tour, he built a focused piece around the exact asset that had caught his eye: how Apollo helps scholars reconstruct ancient papyri, complete with commentary from outside academics and a clear account of why human expertise remains essential when interpreting the model's suggestions.

You cannot manufacture a story like that through pitching alone—the researchers built the model, and the journalist did the reporting. What editorial judgement can do is bridge the gap between the two: looking across a company's technical output to find the single project an outsider will actually care about, packaging the context so it can travel through a newsroom intact, and moving fast enough with real evidence that a reporter can turn initial curiosity into a published piece.
