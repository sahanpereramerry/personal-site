Most executive thought leadership programmes start with a flawed premise. An executive is a cornucopia of knowledge, and all it takes is a few questions from a freelance editor to extract it. It is an intuitive model. After all, who better to articulate a company's perspective than the person who runs it?

In practice, however, this places the heaviest creative burden on the busiest person in the organisation. It takes time-poor leaders, shoves them into a 60-minute meeting, and expects them to spew wisdom that can be neatly formatted into a topical, distinctive and substantiated blog post.

Most cannot do it. This is why most executive LinkedIn accounts either resort to getting their best ideas from Claude, or go silent after three posts.

The problem here is one of methodology. These executives do not lack insight, but trying to extract it tasks them with the wrong job.

Rejecting the ‘extraction’ model

To solve this, we have to invert the usual division of labour. I recently saw this play out while running the thought leadership operation for Kush Wadhwa, the founder of Trilateral Research, a Responsible AI consultancy working across the public and private sectors.

Before we started, his posts were sporadic. Our aim was to position him as a recognised voice among a clear target audience: AI governance researchers, regulators, enterprise technology leaders, and the policy scholars orbiting them. Over three months, we published twelve posts on his LinkedIn. Two reached roughly 40,000 impressions each, drawing engagement from the UK AI Safety Institute, the European Commission's AI Office, IBM researchers, and policy academics at Stanford and Harvard.

We achieved this using what I call the ‘commentary model’. Rather than sitting down with a CEO to mine their best ideas (a process that is both time-consuming and oddly adversarial, as anyone who has attempted it will know), the writer takes full responsibility for the research and positioning.

First, the writer surveys the landscape. Within a field like Responsible AI, this means combing through academic papers, regulatory developments, and industry reports—whatever constitutes the active intellectual frontier. The goal is to identify material that the target audience would find genuinely valuable, but has not had the time to encounter or digest. The writer prepares the foundation: the context, the angle, and the structural argument. The CEO's role, then, is not to generate ideas, but to supply commentary: practitioner judgement, credibility, and the critical ‘so what’ that transforms a research summary into a point of view.

This distinction matters. It fundamentally changes what you ask of the executive's time.

Under the extraction model, every post requires a lengthy interview. Under the commentary model, the writer arrives with a prepared brief—here is the material, here is the proposed angle, here is the draft argument—and the CEO's contribution is purely editorial. They push back on framing, test conclusions against their operational experience, and sharpen the language for their peers. It is a 30-minute conversation, executed with brutal efficiency.

The principle underneath: no one does the reading

The commentary model works because of a structural feature of professional attention. Every field has a substantial body of high-quality material that practitioners would find valuable, if they had time to find it and understand it. But they never have that time. As any busy executive knows, attention is the bottleneck.

To put it simply: there is a massive gulf between the research being published at the frontier of an industry and what is published on social media. LinkedIn is saturated with opinion and thin on substance.

The writer's core function, in this model, is to provide the CEO with this value upfront. The CEO no longer bears the burden of coming up with a completely new take; their job is simply to add the final spin to something that is already valuable.

AI can assist here. For example, there are tools that exist to monitor arXiv for recent papers in a given domain, or to track what is gaining traction on X within a specialist community. However, this system does require a level of subject-matter expertise from the writer, who must be able to engage with any research material properly.

The most successful post in the Trilateral campaign illustrates the point. I had been trying to parse AI governance material from different sectors and kept encountering the same problem: everyone used different terms for identical concepts. ‘Explainability’ to the engineers, ‘transparency’ to the lawyers, ‘interpretability’ to the regulators. When I came across the IBM AI Risk Atlas, I recognised that it mapped precisely the terminology fragmentation I had been struggling with. The angle emerged from that recognition: AI governance does not have a technology problem. It has a language problem.

My first draft leaned too heavily on the academic framing. The CEO’s editorial contribution was to apply the practitioner's test: what would a CTO reading this at seven in the morning actually do with it? We landed on a conclusion that was both specific and actionable: that while the sector's language problem cannot be solved from outside, any organisation can start by getting its own teams to agree on terms. The audience that engaged included Oxbridge PhDs, IBM researchers, policy experts, and CTOs—precisely the community within which Wadhwa needed to be a recognised name.

Measuring reputation, not reach

A thought leadership campaign for a CEO is not a brand awareness exercise. Raw impressions, in isolation, are a vanity metric. The relevant indicators are qualitative: who engages, what happens as a consequence, and whether the audience returns.

In the Trilateral campaign, the meaningful outcomes were engagement from institutional actors in the AI governance space, inbound podcast invitations, and direct messages from the kind of senior figures who do not ordinarily respond to LinkedIn posts. These are the signals that a reputation is forming within a defined community, and they are worth more than any volume of reactions from outside it.

There is also a compounding effect worth noting. Two strong posts published in close succession gave the second significant tailwind from the first. LinkedIn's algorithm rewards consistency, and an audience that has engaged once is more likely to see and engage with the next piece. Protecting that cadence matters as much as the quality of any individual post.

The trust architecture

The commentary model requires something that is easy to describe and difficult to establish: the CEO must trust the writer's domain competence enough to let them lead on topic selection.

This matters more than it might appear. Without that trust, the temptation—particularly in an era of capable AI writing tools—is for the executive to bypass the research grounding entirely, to take a draft and feed it through a rewriter, or to default to whatever topic feels safest rather than whatever topic would be most valuable. The result is content that is polished but undifferentiated.

Practically, this means the writer-executive relationship needs to be established and boundaries need to be set before the first post is written. Who identifies candidate topics? Who owns the angle? Who has final say on the headline when the writer and the executive disagree? What does the feedback loop look like—a marked-up draft, a conversation, a voice note? These are not logistical details. They are the architecture of a creative partnership, and leaving them undefined inevitably introduces friction that compounds over time.

Where these partnerships typically encounter friction is in the ambiguity over final authority—on a headline, for instance, where a writer has data on what performs and an executive has an instinct about what feels right. The lesson from running these campaigns is simple: when you are commissioned to own the strategy, you need explicit space to execute it.

What this model asks of the writer

If this works so well, why do more companies not use it? The answer is simple: the commentary model does not work with a generalist content writer. It requires someone who can read the primary material in a given domain. Not merely summarise a press release about it, but engage with the substance, identify what is genuinely novel or under-discussed, and frame an argument around it that a knowledgeable audience will find credible. Lateral thinking is required to spot when a piece of research neatly maps onto a problem the target audience is invested in.

Let us be clear. This is a higher bar than most thought leadership operations set, and it is why most of them produce forgettable content. The writer who merely transcribes is replaceable—by a cheaper writer, or increasingly by a machine. The writer who does the reading and identifies the angle is not, because that work requires the same kind of judgement that makes the CEO's commentary valuable in the first place.
